# Phone habits, existing products and barriers for informal workers (research briefs, 16 November 2026)

Gathered by AI research agents with web search; every finding carries its source. Treat as a starting point and check sources before citing.

## Brief 1
Across 2020-2026 the products that actually reached very informal workers ran on the channels they already had: USSD/SMS (Biscate, Lynk's yes/no SMS job offers), IVR voice (Viamo), zero-rated web plus a toll-free call centre and WhatsApp (SA Youth), and WhatsApp bots (Coachmee, Farmer.Chat, GiveDirectly's Rwanda pilot). The strongest independent outcome evidence is Biscate's World Bank study (incomes more than doubled for surveyed workers), but gains were concentrated in roughly 20% of users and the binding constraint was job scarcity, not matching. Lynk and Fuzu show that open marketplaces for low-skill work stall on slow quoting, unreliable workers, mistrust and thin demand; what worked was standardising the job, pre-paying, and letting the platform brand carry trust. Generative-AI pilots with poor users (Farmer.Chat, GiveDirectly, Viamo) found heavy, late-night, broad use, but voice notes were slow and unreliable, local-language quality was weak, low-literacy users struggled to phrase questions, and trust came from cited sources and visible results. Skills elicitation from informal experience (Tabiya Compass with Harambee, J-PAL Kenya RCT) is the live research frontier but has no results yet. Two cross-cutting pain points stand out for ideation: WhatsApp job scams that charge "registration fees", and fragile phone-number-plus-mobile-money identity (Kazi Mtaani payment failures, Fuliza deductions). AI that verifies offers, standardises a worker's offer, or turns voice into a sourced record fits these constraints better than another job board.

- Biscate (Mozambique, UX Information Technologies, since 2016) is a USSD job-matching service for informal workers: workers register by menu on a basic phone (category, name, city, experience, education), customers browse profiles and get a phone number, and workers are notified by SMS. Almost 70,000 workers registered (mostly under 35), 314,000+ customer inquiries, ~24,000 completed jobs across 18 occupations; free to users via a telecom partnership, roughly US$1.2m/year flows to workers. A 2021 World Bank study found surveyed workers' monthly income more than doubled on average, but UX's own data shows ~20% of users earned ten times more while many saw little change. Problems: ranking favours long-registered workers (testing boosts for newcomers), ratings polluted by people the worker never served, low awareness, and limited rural reach (<10% smartphone ownership in pilot areas). (https://www.foodfortransformation.org/full-article/mozambique-how-informal-workers-find-jobs-through-an-app.html, 2016-2023) — The clearest proof that feature-phone, no-data job matching for informal workers can work at scale and raise income; also shows the key failure modes (incumbent bias, fake ratings, job scarcity) an AI layer could target.
- Lynk (Nairobi, 2015-2022, acquired by Eden Life) pivoted three times. Open quoting (v1) failed: quotes were slow, inconsistent and inflated after Pros saw the customer's home, new Pros could not win against rated ones, Pros took jobs outside their skills and subcontracted. Standardised fixed-price services with pre-payment (v2) worked better: Pros got SMS yes/no job offers and were assigned automatically, customers got a Pro within two hours, new Pros got work within hours of signing up, and trust shifted from the individual to the Lynk brand. Reliability remained a problem (good Pros 'go mteja', become unreachable); vetting and training were costly and vocational partnerships failed, so Lynk built its own academy. Similar Kenyan platforms KibaruaNow and Taskwetu shut down. (https://theflip.africa/newsletter/lynk-lessons-learned-the-hard-way/, 2015-2022) — Shows that for informal trades the hard problems are standardising the offer, pricing, reliability and customer mistrust, not discovery. An AI that helps a worker state a standard, priced offer and keeps a reliability record addresses what killed v1.
- SA Youth (Harambee, South Africa) is zero-rated and supports young work-seekers through WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, email and a toll-free contact centre handling ~150,000 calls a month. Over 5 million registered, 1.4-1.7 million 'connected to earning opportunities' (definition not stated), 2,800-3,500 employers, 65% women, only 1% people with disabilities. 'Inclusive' CVs highlight volunteering and informal work. Harambee names high data costs as a key blocker that makes work-seekers curtail or abandon online job search. (https://trialogueknowledgehub.co.za/handbook-2025-harambee-sa-youth-connecting-millions-of-south-african-youth-to-opportunities/, 2025) — The largest youth-employment channel in Yoma's core market relies on a human call centre plus WhatsApp and zero-rating, not an app; any AI use case must be data-free or WhatsApp-native and complement human guides.
- Harambee's Coachmee (announced July 2023) is a WhatsApp chatbot integrated with the sayouth.mobi contact centre that gives work-seekers goal-setting, check-ins, affirmations and motivation alongside human guides. Reported 6,000+ registered users and 37,610 coaching conversations in its first year, grown mainly by word of mouth; 97% of users who reached 20 check-ins called it their main accountability partner; 15 work-seekers attributed a job to it. Separately, Harambee reports AI-powered multichannel support with 92% first-contact resolution (Genesys case study, 4.5m work-seekers supported in 2024) and an AI21-built onboarding chatbot that raised programme sign-ups by 20%. All figures are self-reported or vendor-reported. (https://www.harambee.co.za/coachmee-ai-powered-guidance-and-support-for-unemployed-south-african-youth/, 2023-2024) — Evidence that a WhatsApp AI coach is adopted by unemployed youth when framed as support around a human service; uptake is modest relative to platform size, so the job itself (motivation, onboarding) matters more than the bot.
- Tabiya's open-source Compass is a multi-agent LLM 'interviewer' that elicits skills from paid, unpaid, informal and volunteer experience and maps them to an inclusive taxonomy. A World Bank/Oxford study with Harambee and SAYouth.mobi (deck dated Sept 2025) is testing AI-supported skills elicitation and matching in South Africa; a J-PAL randomised evaluation in Kenya with Tabiya and Swahilipot compares control, human counsellor only, AI only, and human plus AI arms on job search and employment for marginalised job seekers. No outcome results published yet. The deck notes only ~8% of demanded skills in entry-level SAYouth.mobi postings are soft skills. (https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative-project/learning-recommend-ai-human-counselors-and-job-matching-kenya, 2025) — This is the closest existing work to YoCV: conversational skill discovery from informal experience is being rigorously tested right now in Yoma's markets, so a prototype should differentiate (channel, voice, verification) rather than duplicate.
- Giraffe (South Africa, 2015) is a free mobisite (not an app) where jobseekers build a CV on their phone or sign up by SMS shortcode; employers pay to post. Matching algorithms contact candidates, who are screened and asked to record a voice note explaining their suitability, which employers listen to. Over 1 million jobseekers and ~3,000 businesses; UNICEF Innovation Fund backed open-sourcing parts of the matching algorithm. (https://www.unicef.org/innovation/innovation-fund-giraffe, 2015-2021) — A voice note as the screening artefact, and SMS sign-up, are proven low-friction patterns for low-literacy, low-data job seekers in South Africa.
- SweepSouth's annual domestic worker surveys (5,500-8,300 responses, circulated by SMS and WhatsApp, reverse-billed so no data was needed) show platform workers' median monthly earnings at R4,999 (2023) and R5,242 (2024) against R2,992 and R3,404 off-platform, with off-platform pay below the minimum wage. Around one in ten respondents reported phone problems (no charging during loadshedding, lost signal) that stopped them contacting clients, navigating, or confirming and closing bookings; 35% are in debt, half of them to a shop. (https://static.sweepsouth.com/blog/sixth-annual-domestic-workers-report-on-pay-and-working-conditions-for-domestic-workers-in-south-africa/, 2023-2024) — Shows the earnings premium of formalised bookings and ratings for domestic work, and that app-dependent flows break on cheap phones and power cuts; reverse-billed SMS/WhatsApp reached the workers.
- Fuzu (Kenya/Uganda, 8.5 million users, free for job seekers, paid by employers) ran a Finnfund-backed mobile career project in Uganda's Bidi Bidi refugee camp: of 1,000 participants offered support, only about 40 were employed by the end, which the funder called 'not high'. Fuzu has since launched an 'AI Powered Job Connections Program' for disadvantaged youth and, with GIZ, a Digital Matching Platform for users with limited digital access (Feb 2026, no results). (https://www.finnfund.fi/en/news-and-publications/blog/mobile-career-boost-what-we-learned-from-the-bidi-bidi-refugee-camp/, 2020-2026) — A large job board with AI matching still converts poorly for the most marginalised when local demand is thin; matching is not the bottleneck for people far from formal jobs.
- Jobberman (Nigeria, Mastercard Foundation Young Africa Works) delivered soft-skills training and placement at scale (40,000-50,000+ trained/placed per vendor and programme counts, targets of 5 million trained and 3 million placed). Evidence is a midline evaluation and alumni surveys: 97% rated training relevant and 55% of surveyed beneficiaries reported getting a job, with no comparison group. Rigorous Nigerian RCTs on skills come from other programmes (e.g. Crawford et al. 2021: skills training raised self-employment 14 points and profits 38%). (https://nairametrics.com/2023/11/06/do-soft-skills-really-matter/, 2020-2023) — Training-plus-platform models report big numbers but little causal evidence; informal workers may gain more from self-employment support than from formal placement.
- Digital Green's Farmer.Chat (WhatsApp, Telegram, app; Swahili, Amharic, Hausa, Hindi, Odiya, Telugu, English) lets farmers send voice notes that are transcribed, translated to English for a RAG pipeline and answered with text and TTS audio. 15,000+ users and 300,000+ queries (Kenya: 8,805 users, 225,500+ queries, ~29 per user; top 35% of users send ~80% of queries). About 75% of queries are answered; of the rest 66% fail for missing content. RAGAS faithfulness ~80% high, relevance 67% high; average response 9 s. Low-digital-literacy users struggled to phrase questions (clarity ~2.25/3); clickable follow-up questions drove 45% of interactions; 20-30 s video tutorials boosted adoption; trust rose when answers cited the Ministry of Agriculture and when results showed on farmers' own land; women and older farmers faced smartphone and data barriers. (https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08916v2, 2024) — The best-documented WhatsApp voice-in/voice-out GenAI service for low-literacy users in Kenya; its design lessons (suggested replies, short video onboarding, sourced answers, translate-to-English pipeline) transfer directly to a jobs or income assistant.
- GiveDirectly gave 832 rural Rwandan cash-transfer recipients unrestricted access to a ChatGPT-powered WhatsApp chatbot (Nov 2025-Apr 2026, ~21,000 inbound messages). People asked about spending the transfer and business advice, then family conflict, child health, market prices and personal questions; use peaked late at night. Failures: Kinyarwanda support in major LLMs is inconsistent and often poor, voice notes (the natural interface for lower-literacy users) were slow and unreliable, and answers about rural markets and services were weak. Red-flag messages were routed to the call centre. A similar pilot is running in Malawi; next step is an A/B test of cash vs cash plus chatbot. (https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/pzFa3GGWfYLPNEGpq/what-21-000-whatsapp-messages-reveal-about-ai-utility-in, 2025-2026) — Direct evidence of how very poor WhatsApp users actually use a general AI (heavily, broadly, at night) and the three things that break: local language, voice, and local knowledge.
- Viamo's 'Ask Viamo Anything' (Zambia, from May 2023, with MTN) put generative AI behind an IVR line so basic-phone users could ask questions by voice in local languages. First two weeks: 500 users, 489 questions, 453 valid answers (93%), 59% women; users quickly learned to rephrase prompts. GSMA wrote it up in March 2025; Viamo said it would not sell it as a standard product yet and is rolling out to Pakistan, India, Nigeria and Tanzania; a later 'offline voice GenAI for basic phones' was announced. (https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/gsma_resources/voice-first-generative-ai-for-impact-insights-from-viamos-ask-viamo-anything-pilot-in-zambia/, 2023-2025) — Shows a voice-call GenAI channel for feature-phone users is feasible and reaches women, but remains a small pilot; a toll-free voice line is an option for workers who do not use WhatsApp.
- WhatsApp-first AI job assistants have appeared across southern Africa: Jobot (Zimbabwe) claims 12,000+ job seekers helped with AI CV building, matching and applying, free for seekers; Sebenzo (South Africa) is a hackathon bot that builds a CV from WhatsApp input; ChatSA sells CV tailoring and interview prep for R45-R1,100. None has an independent evaluation. On the employer side, South African recruiters use WhatsApp screening bots (matric, SA ID, shift availability) as a first filter for high-volume roles, and vendor guidance itself warns such screening struggles with matric-only candidates, informal work histories and multilingual applicants and can screen people out on digital literacy rather than job skills. (https://shiftmate.co.za/resources/blog/recruitment-tech-ai-in-gauteng-hiring, 2023-2026) — The CV-bot space is already crowded and unproven; the under-served side is helping informal workers survive employer-side WhatsApp screening and present informal histories.
- Fake job offers on WhatsApp and Facebook are endemic in South Africa and Kenya: scammers rotate numbers and names, offer paid 'tasks', or impersonate retailers (Naivas, Quickmart, Woolworths, Capitec), hospitals and government bodies and demand 'registration', 'hiring' or 'medical check' fees; Kenyan police arrested four people who took millions of shillings for non-existent jobs. Fact-checkers' core rule: a real employer never charges to apply, interview, train, get a uniform or be processed. Victims rarely recover money. (https://africacheck.org/tags/job-scam?page=1, 2023-2026) — Scam detection is a concrete, high-value AI use case on the exact channel these workers use: forward a job message and get a verdict and the 'never pay' rule; it also matches YoCard's existing 'Never pay before the work is done' line.
- Kazi Mtaani (Kenya, 2020-2022) employed 280,000+ youth in informal settlements on public works, recruited by registration with national ID and paid by M-Pesa through Safaricom. Payment failed for many because details were captured wrongly or did not match (youth told to resubmit to county committees), some underage youth registered with fake IDs, and outstanding Fuliza overdrafts were auto-deducted from wages, so recruits registered with other people's phone numbers. The World Bank credited it with reducing crime and dependence, but no rigorous 2023-2024 evaluation was found. (https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2020-08-12-how-fake-ids-m-pesa-lock-youths-out-of-kazi-mtaani-payments, 2020-2022) — Shows that phone number plus mobile money is a fragile identity and payment rail for informal workers (shared SIMs, debt deductions, mismatched records); any card or credential tied to a phone number needs to anticipate this.
- Kenya has an estimated 1.5 million gig workers and a ~US$1 billion gig market; most ride-hailing and platform pay flows through M-Pesa, which gives informal workers a verified, timestamped record of economic activity, and commentators argue platforms largely digitise existing informal work rather than create jobs. A 2025 study of Mathare informal settlement found advanced fintech, gig and utility platforms are rarely used there, citing cost and lack of formal IDs; IDinsight finds boda and delivery platform work does not reliably give stable income or social protection. (https://tech-ish.com/2026/03/23/africa-informal-economy-went-digital-gig, 2025-2026) — Mobile-money transaction history is an existing, trusted proof-of-work source for informal workers; but the poorest settlements still sit outside platforms because of cost and ID, which argues for consent-based use of M-Pesa/SMS records rather than new onboarding.

## Brief 2
The strongest documented barriers for informal workers and unemployed youth in sub-Saharan Africa are not the lack of a CV but (1) two-sided information frictions: employers and customers cannot see a worker's skills or reliability, and workers cannot credibly signal them (Carranza et al. 2022; Abel, Burger and Piraino 2020 showed one reference letter raised callbacks 60% and doubled women's employment; Biscate workers with even one client comment got 13x more requests); (2) cash cost of search, dominated by transport (median R550/month, R350 of it transport; R600 outlay before a first pay cheque) and data (1GB often above 7% of monthly income, 42% usage gap in the region, 60% on 3G or feature phones); (3) non-payment, fee reneging and harassment, worst for women doing roadside day labour and domestic work (1 in 5 domestic workers feel unsafe, 39% paid below minimum wage); (4) fake job adverts on Facebook and WhatsApp that extract fees and personal details; (5) language and interview screening in English when township schooling and home language differ, with employers themselves saying soft skills matter most but cannot be observed. Youth also say their top barrier is lack of work experience (35%), yet 23% want to start a business, the second-lowest in Africa. The two repo prototypes already address signalling (YoCV, YoCard) with grounding checks and a WhatsApp-openable card, but neither works over voice notes, in local languages, over USSD or on feature phones, and neither touches scam detection, payment disputes, customer-side vetting, or transport-aware search. The most promising AI use cases live in WhatsApp voice and text: a scam checker, a reference-request helper, a pay agreement and reminder record, a local-language interview rehearsal, and a customer-side trust lookup.

- The median unemployed young South African spends about R550 a month on job search, R350 of it on transport and R200 on printing, data and other costs; Harambee estimates a newly hired young person must pay about R600 in transport before the first pay cheque. Transport is the single largest search cost, driven by apartheid-era spatial layouts that place poor households far from jobs. (https://harambee.co.za/breaking-barriers-november-2017/ and https://justjobsnetwork.org/files/policy-implications-for-a-mobile-workforce-transport-costs-as-a-barrier-to-employment_oct-2018.pdf, 2017-2018) — An AI tool should avoid sending people to physical places unnecessarily: pre-screen by WhatsApp voice or text, cluster nearby opportunities, estimate taxi fare before a trip, and flag listings whose transport cost exceeds a day's pay. Harambee's Uber and Bolt voucher pilots show demand for a transport-aware layer.
- In the Addis Ababa RCT, a bus-fare subsidy raised job-search intensity and made recipients 25% more likely to be in formal employment, but the effect faded; a cheap job-application workshop that taught people to present their skills produced lasting gains in earnings, job satisfaction and job duration. (https://g2lm-lic.iza.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/GLMLIC-Policy-Brief_022-1.pdf, 2016-2020) — Helping people present what they can do is more durable than paying for travel. A conversational AI coach that rehearses how to describe your experience to a customer or employer, in your own language, is the scalable version of that workshop.
- Carranza, Garlick, Orkin and Rankin assessed 6,891 Johannesburg work-seekers and gave them certificates of numeracy, communication, grit and planning. Employers cannot observe these skills, so certificates raised employment and earnings, and work-seekers updated their beliefs and search strategy. 97% had searched the week before (average 17 hours) and only 9% had ever held a long-term job. (https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20200961 and https://www.harambee.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/job-search-AER.pdf, 2022) — Credible, third-party-checkable signals of skill move both employers and workers. An AI tool could run short voice-based skill checks (e.g. spoken customer-service scenarios, basic arithmetic for a spaza till) and record results as a Yoma-style credential a customer can look up, extending YoCard's 'confirmed' tier beyond completed Yoma opportunities.
- Abel, Burger and Piraino found that adding a reference letter to a South African job application raised employer callbacks from 4.2% to 6.7% (about 60%), and that encouraging job seekers to obtain letters doubled employment among women, closing the gender gap in their sample. Letters were rarely used because workers did not ask for them and past employers rarely wrote them. (https://conference.iza.org/conference_files/GLMLICNetwork_2017/abel_m25817.pdf, 2020) — Most informal workers have past customers who would vouch for them but no mechanism to collect it. An AI assistant on WhatsApp could draft a one-tap reference request in the customer's language, send it, and turn the customer's voice-note reply into a short structured reference attached to the worker's card. Women benefit most.
- On Mozambique's Biscate platform (USSD for workers, so it runs on any phone; app for clients), the average active worker's monthly profit doubled in cohort 1 and rose 67% in cohort 2 during a fiscal crisis. By February 2020 only 15% of active workers had a client comment on their profile, but those workers received 13 times more service requests than workers with no comment. Age, gender and education did not predict success; tenure in the trade did. (https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/jobs/how-app-helping-mozambiques-informal-service-workers-earn-more and https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/933731622622709586/pdf/Study-of-the-Biscate-Jobs-Platform-in-Mozambique-World-Bank-Let-s-Work-Program-Mozambique.pdf, 2016-2021) — Proof that feature-phone-first matching works in a Yoma country and that one piece of customer feedback is enormously valuable. An AI tool should make it trivial for a customer to leave a rating by replying to a WhatsApp or SMS prompt, and should work over USSD or SMS for workers without smartphones.
- Job scams are endemic where unemployment is high: Code for Africa found about 30 Facebook accounts, groups and pages with over 184,000 followers pushing fake job ads to Kenyans in 2020; a Nigerian victim paid N25,000 then was asked N92,000 more for 'training kits'; WhatsApp job scams in South Africa impersonate real agencies, use rotating numbers, promise pay for YouTube subscriptions or hotel ratings, and harvest personal details. Red flags are short deadlines, high pay with no qualifications, and links to external forms. (https://nation.africa/africa/news/how-online-scammers-are-targeting-kenyan-job-seekers-3885924 and https://www.news24.com/fin24/news24/tech-and-trends/whatsapp-scammers-are-offering-fake-jobs-online-in-efforts-to-swindle-victims-personal-details-for-money-20231214, 2020-2023) — A 'forward it to check it' scam detector on WhatsApp: the user forwards a job message or screenshot and an AI replies in their language with a plain verdict and the specific red flags, cross-checking the organisation against Yoma's live opportunity and organisation lists. Harambee's SA Youth support team already does this manually over WhatsApp and Messenger.
- Women waiting roadside for casual domestic work in East London (Amalinda junction) report employers reneging on agreed fees, dodging wages after month-long sleep-in jobs, firing without pay, and men offering R250 to R400 for sex while they wait; the Labour Department said it cannot help with harassment. Workers asked for a desk to record employers' details and vehicle registrations. (https://elitshanews.org.za/?p=13859, 2020) — The workers themselves asked for a record of who hired them. An AI-assisted WhatsApp 'job note' could capture by voice note the employer's name, number plate, agreed fee and dates, time-stamp it, and generate a polite payment reminder or a dispute summary later. A check-in message at the end of a sleep-in job adds a safety signal.
- SweepSouth's 2025 survey of over 5,000 South African domestic workers (via SMS and WhatsApp, June-July 2025) found 39% paid below the R28.79 hourly minimum wage, median take-home about R3,635 against R4,500 living costs, 1 in 5 feeling unsafe in an employer's home citing verbal, physical or sexual abuse, 85% working part-time across several households, and 82% breadwinners. Rest of World separately reported clients asking platform cleaners for unpaid extra hours. (https://iol.co.za/sunday-tribune/news/2025-09-07-working-but-still-in-crisis/ and https://sweepsouth.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025_DomesticWorkerReportV3.pdf, 2025) — Workers juggling several households need help tracking what was agreed with whom. An AI assistant could keep a per-customer agreement (hours, rate, tasks) from a voice note, tell the worker when a request falls below minimum wage or outside the agreement, and draft the message that says so. The SMS/WhatsApp survey method also proves these workers answer on those channels.
- GSMA reports a 42% mobile internet usage gap among adults in sub-Saharan Africa, an entry-level handset costing 87% of monthly income for the poorest 20%, 1GB of data often above 7% of monthly income, 60% of mobile internet users on older 3G devices or feature phones, and women 29% less likely than men to use mobile internet. (https://www.gsma.com/somic/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/The-State-of-Mobile-Internet-Connectivity-2025-Affordability-of-Internet-Enabled-Handsets-and-Data.pdf and https://www.gsma.com/mobileeconomy/sub-saharan-africa/, 2025) — Any AI use case must be text-light or voice-based over WhatsApp, with an SMS or USSD fallback, zero-rated where possible, and must not assume an app install. Short replies, no images, and a once-a-day digest rather than chat-style back and forth keep data cost near zero.
- WhatsApp voice-note AI advisers already run in Yoma-adjacent markets: UlangiziAI in Malawi answered over 4,000 questions in Chichewa and English in three months, Mshauri in Kenya works in Swahili, AgriVoice in Ghana takes Asante Twi voice notes. Pilots report that some users had a local agent read the answer aloud, that early local-language synthetic voices sounded strange, and that mainstream models' voice support for African languages is often unreliable. (https://restofworld.org/2025/malawi-ulangizi-ai-farming-chatbot and https://www.bmz-digital.global/en/how-ai-is-transforming-farming-advice/, 2024-2025) — The interaction pattern (voice note in, short voice or text out, human escalation when unsure) is proven and transferable from farming to work. Build in a human reviewer path and local-language speech checks; do not rely on synthetic voice quality in isiZulu, Kiswahili or Portuguese without testing.
- English proficiency raises employment probability and wages in South Africa (one estimate: a 33% wage premium per unit of proficiency), and dominant-language skill lowers the information cost of job search; yet township schools teach in English with weak quality, and Harambee advises employers that interviewing in a language that is not the candidate's mother tongue holds back fully capable people. (https://open.uct.ac.za/handle/11427/37453 and https://www.harambee.co.za/advice-from-a-former-job-seeker-for-unemployed-young-people/, 2010-2023) — An AI intermediary can let the worker speak in their home language and present them to a customer or employer in English (or vice versa), with the original kept as the source of truth. The repo's testing log already shows isiZulu and Kiswahili input breaking the template parser, so translation-first design is a concrete next step.
- Oxford's Mind and Behaviour group found that firms say soft skills matter most for young hires but cannot observe them before employment; Harambee's spoken-English assessment predicts employment and earnings; screening large applicant pools is costly because skill signals are imprecise. Harambee and the J-PAL study both gave interview preparation (how to prepare and dress), about an hour of counselling. (https://mbrg.bsg.ox.ac.uk/mind-and-behaviour-projects/can-matching-soft-skills-yield-better-firm-worker-matches and https://www.iol.co.za/business-report/careers/harambee-says-sa-youth-needs-more-than-qualifications-for-the-job-market-29422751, 2020-2023) — A WhatsApp voice-note mock interview or 'practise pitching your service' coach lets someone rehearse in private, get plain feedback, and record a short spoken self-introduction a customer can play from the card. It addresses interview anxiety without naming it and gives the employer a soft-skill signal.
- Afrobarometer's 2025 South Africa survey (n=1,600, June-July 2025) finds 47% of 18-35s unemployed and looking for work, 20% not looking; youth name lack of work experience (35%) and inadequate training (25%) as the main barriers; only 23% would like to start their own business (second-lowest of 38 countries, African average 52%) and 45% would prefer government work. Statistics SA puts youth unemployment at 45.5% in 2024. (https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/AD1068-South-Africa-youth-see-government-as-failing-on-job-creation-Afrobarometer-31oct25.pdf, 2025) — Informal work is often the only work available but is not seen as 'experience' or as a business. An AI tool that reframes car washing, braiding or piece jobs as evidence of experience (what YoCV does) and that helps price and schedule the work (nudging toward self-employment) tackles both the 'no experience' belief and the entrepreneurship gap.
- Nearly nine in ten workers in sub-Saharan Africa are informally employed (ILOSTAT 87.6%), nearly half of employed youth are working poor, and households of about six in ten workers live in poverty; the region added 15.4 million to its labour force in 2025 against 14.6 million jobs created. A World Bank working paper finds mobile phone access is high across all job types, and a Ghana/Malawi/South Africa study concludes phone access alone does not solve the search problem. (https://ilostat.ilo.org/blog/the-world-of-work-and-the-2030-agenda-a-ten-year-review/ and https://theconversation.com/access-to-mobile-phones-wont-magically-fix-youth-unemployment-in-africa-88786, 2024-2026) — The target population is the majority, not an edge case, and already has phones; the missing piece is what the phone does for income. AI value lies in reducing specific frictions (verification, payment, scams, language) rather than in another job board.
- Gig and informal workers in Johannesburg and Nairobi vet clients through WhatsApp groups and fellow freelancers after being cheated, form informal unions mainly on Facebook and WhatsApp, and small Johannesburg rubble-removal contractors still find work through handwritten posters, street-corner advertising, Facebook comments and word of mouth; one said the biggest problem is simply finding steady jobs. (https://writeafrica.siasaplace.com/working-for-the-ghost/ and https://pressportal.co.za/energy-and-environment/story/b8hkokn8nerbbp4pmn2p-20260520.html, 2024-2026) — Trust already flows through peer WhatsApp groups. An AI tool could sit inside those groups: a group member forwards a customer's number or a job offer and gets back whether that customer has prior non-payment reports from other workers (with consent and privacy limits), and workers can pool and share a reputation record. This is the customer-side mirror of YoCard.
- The two repo prototypes already cover: turning a plain description of informal work into reviewed CV claims with server-side grounding checks that reject unsupported numbers and credential claims (YoCV), and a WhatsApp-openable public work card with 'Confirmed from Yoma' and 'Stated by you' tiers, a signed record, masked phone, 'never pay before the work is done' notice and one-tap withdraw (YoCard). Known gaps recorded in the testing log: English-only generation, no language detection, isiZulu and Kiswahili price ranges mis-parsed, no voice input, no USSD or SMS path, no scam or payment features, under-18 gate relies on self-declared date of birth, and the live model path was never exercised. (/Users/nathanparis/Downloads/yocv-prototype/README.md and /Users/nathanparis/Downloads/yocv-prototype/docs/testing-log.md, 2026) — New use cases should extend rather than duplicate: add voice-note and local-language input in front of the existing grounding checks, a feature-phone (SMS/USSD) rendering of the card, customer-side reference capture feeding the 'confirmed' tier, and sibling WhatsApp flows for scam checking and pay agreements that reuse the same signing and consent model.

## Brief 3
The target users sit squarely in Africa's "usage gap": GSMA (2026) says 63% of Africans are covered by mobile broadband but not online, an entry-level handset costs 24% of monthly income (up to 80% for the poorest), and digital skills/literacy is the second barrier. Device mix is shifting fast but feature phones remain large: Kenya still had ~30m active feature phones against ~45m smartphones in late 2025; Nigeria had 67m feature-phone lines in Sept 2024 and 2G still carries ~38% of connections (2025); South Africa's cheapest prepaid smartphone fell to R399 and feature-phone sales collapsed 87% after the April 2025 tax cut. Among informal workers specifically, Afrobarometer finds only 49% of unskilled manual workers/traders and 17% of people with no formal education own a smartphone. Where people are online, WhatsApp is near-universal (92-95% of internet users in all three countries), voice notes are a daily habit for low-literacy and non-English speakers (isiXhosa township study), and Facebook/WhatsApp groups are where informal jobs circulate (about 850 SA WhatsApp job groups; 45% of Nigerian PWDs job-search via WhatsApp), alongside heavy scam exposure. Work is still found mostly offline: word of mouth (68% of Philippi informal businesses), friends/family, street-corner hiring sites, multiple part-time employers. Data is bought in tiny time-boxed bundles (Kenya KSh20-25/hour; roughly half of Safaricom users use <1GB/month), Nigerian 1GB prices rose 50% in 2025, and SA youth spend R550-R1,000+/month on job search. Mobile money/USSD is the one "app" everyone has (Kenya 82% uptake, 53% daily; Nigerian agents 54%), and zero-rating exists but is patchy (Airtel Nigeria zero-rates Yoma; SA PBO zero-rating is a licence condition due Jan 2027 with only 15-40 sites live). Design implications: WhatsApp-first, voice-first, local-language, tiny data footprint, USSD/SMS fallback, scam-resistant, and anchored in existing word-of-mouth networks.

- Africa-wide (GSMA Mobile Economy Africa 2026): 63% of the population is covered by mobile broadband but not using mobile internet (the 'usage gap'), vs 28% connected and 9% uncovered. An entry-level internet-enabled handset costs 24% of monthly income on average in LMICs, rising to as much as 80% for the poorest. GSMA names affordability as the primary constraint and 'digital skills and literacy' as the second major barrier, particularly for people aware of mobile internet but not yet using it. (https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-economy/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/The-Mobile-Economy-Africa-2026.pdf, 2026 (data for 2025)) — Most of the target users are 'covered but not online' or marginally online; cost of device and data, not network coverage, is the gate. Any AI tool must assume a cheap or shared device, minimal data and low digital confidence, and should degrade to voice/SMS/USSD.
- Kenya (Communications Authority, Q1 FY2025/26, Jul-Sep 2025): 75.0 million connected phones (143.1% penetration); smartphones were 59.5% of connected devices (smartphone penetration 85.2%, feature-phone penetration 57.9%), i.e. roughly 30 million feature phones still active. Mobile money subscriptions were 48.6 million (92.8% penetration). Average mobile broadband user consumed 14.3 GB in the quarter (~4.8 GB/month). Safaricom's 'Maisha Poa Ni Digital' device-financing campaign had sold 2 million 4G phones by May 2025, lifting smartphone share on its network only from 44% to 50% of connections (GSMA 2026). (https://www.ca.go.ke/node/908, 2025) — Even in the most 'mobile-advanced' of the three markets, about 4 in 10 active phones are feature phones, and the half of Safaricom's base that is lowest-income is exactly where informal workers sit. A Kenya-first design needs a USSD/SMS channel in addition to WhatsApp.
- Nigeria: NCC data show smartphones overtook feature phones only in September 2024 (87.32m smartphone vs 67.31m feature-phone lines out of 154.63m). 2G still carried 38.47% of connections in June 2025 (down from 59.32% in Nov 2023) and ~35.9% in April 2026. A 2025 KPMG/Orange survey of 13,251 respondents in 12 cities put smartphone ownership at 75% (up from 64% in 2023) and feature phones at 28% (from 36%); GSMA reports only 39% of rural Nigerians have smartphones vs 73% urban, and 40% of Nigerian women use basic feature phones vs 29% of men. (https://businessday.ng/pro/article/nigerias-smartphone-adoption-surpasses-feature-phones-first-time-ever, 2024-2026) — Roughly a third to 40% of Nigerian lines, concentrated among rural users and women, are on 2G feature phones that cannot run WhatsApp. A job-finding AI reaching these users must have a voice-call or USSD path, and urban survey numbers (75% smartphone) overstate access for informal workers.
- South Africa: 93.8% of households are cellphone-only and 3.7% (~740,000) have no phone (Stats SA GHS 2025). 82.1% of households had internet from any location in 2024 but only 17.4% at home; 75.6% access via mobile (ICASA citing GHS 2024). ICASA counts 83.0m smartphone subscriptions against 117.3m mobile subscriptions at Sept 2025 (~71%); the cheapest prepaid smartphone fell from R499 to R399 in 2025. After the 9% excise duty on sub-R2,500 smartphones was removed on 1 April 2025, entry-level smartphone sales rose 80% and feature-phone sales fell 87% between March 2025 and February 2026 (GSMA). (https://www.icasa.org.za/uploads/files/The-State-of-the-ICT-Sector-Report-of-South-Africa-31-March-2026.pdf, 2024-2026) — South Africa is the market where a smartphone/WhatsApp-first design is most defensible: near-universal household cellphones, cheap entry smartphones and a rapid shift away from feature phones. But internet is mobile-only and prepaid, so data cost per interaction still matters.
- Afrobarometer Round 9 (39 countries, 2021/23, published Aug 2025): 82% of adults own a mobile phone but only 47% own a smartphone and 35% own a basic phone; just 31% use the internet daily. Smartphone ownership by group: unskilled manual worker/trader/retail 49%, agricultural worker 20%, no formal education 17%, primary education 27%, 'high lived poverty' 34%, rural 32% vs urban 64%. Share using the internet daily or a few times a week: South Africa 69%, Kenya 48%, Nigeria 36%. (https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/PP95-Digital-divide-in-Africa-closing-but-participation-in-digitalised-economy-still-uneven-Afrobarometer-23aug25.pdf, 2025 (survey 2021-2023)) — This is the closest population-level view of the actual target group: about half of traders/manual workers and under a fifth of those without schooling have a smartphone. Any 'app' or web flow excludes half the segment; frequent internet use is a minority behaviour outside South Africa.
- Afrobarometer AD1132 (Feb 2026; 2024 phone survey of 1,200 internet-using adults in each of 7 countries incl. Kenya and Nigeria): 85% access the internet mainly through a personal mobile phone (Nigeria 92%, Kenya 84%); 84% of internet users go online daily or a few times a week (Nigeria 92%, Kenya 84%); 82% get news daily/weekly from social media such as Facebook and WhatsApp (Nigeria 89%). Across 38 countries in Round 10 (2024/25), 50% of all adults use the internet daily or a few times a week. (https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/AD1132-Africans-increasingly-online-but-digital-safety-remains-low-Afrobarometer-20feb26.pdf, 2026 (survey 2024)) — For those who are online, the phone is the only computer and Facebook/WhatsApp are the information feed, including for news and (by extension) work. Information about jobs has to travel through those feeds rather than a separate portal.
- WhatsApp is near-universal among internet users: Statista-based figures put monthly WhatsApp use at 95.4% of internet users in South Africa, 93.5% in Kenya and 91.9% in Nigeria. GeoPoll's July 2025 survey (3,945 respondents in Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda; skews young/urban) found WhatsApp 90%, Facebook 82%, TikTok 72%, and 64% use WhatsApp 'for business purposes'; 35% spend 3-6 hours/day on social media. A 2025 four-country study (Kenya, Nigeria, India, Pakistan) found 94% of connected smartphone users had WhatsApp, 58% of self-employed Nigerian women (65% of men) use it for livelihood activity, and about 30% of qualitative participants in Kenya and Nigeria used shared phones. (https://www.geopoll.com/blog/smartphone-and-social-media-usage-2025/, 2025-2026) — WhatsApp (and Facebook) is the only channel with near-total reach among connected informal workers; it is already the de facto business tool. Shared phones (~30%) mean identity, privacy and 'one phone, many workers' must be designed for.
- Voice notes are a daily, literacy-bridging practice. In a CHI 2022 study with isiXhosa speakers in Langa/Khayelitsha (Cape Town), all participants reported sending WhatsApp voice messages 'every day, without fail'; voice avoids the cost and difficulty of typing unabbreviated isiXhosa (no predictive dictionaries, norms against abbreviation), lets 'everyone clearly understand the message', and users often delete voice notes right after sending because they cannot search them. Jobberman Nigeria now delivers all its WhatsApp training as 'voiced' content with graphics at roughly 200 MB per course, explicitly for marginalised job seekers. A South African WhatsApp health study found recipients rated voice notes easier to understand than SMS. (https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa59573/Download/59573__22666__12859841e0c34bc9949e1c476fc39f76.pdf, 2022-2025) — Speech in local languages is the natural input for this group, not typed English. An AI that listens to a voice note, extracts experience/skills and talks back in the same language removes the literacy and typing barriers; it also must handle code-switching (isiXhosa/English, Swahili/Sheng, Pidgin).
- How informal businesses actually communicate and market (HSRC Innovation in the Informal Sector Survey, Philippi township, Cape Town, 2021-22, published 2025): 83.09% use a cellular phone as their main communication facility; 32.89% use WhatsApp to communicate with customers and 24.95% Facebook; primary marketing is word of mouth (68.04%) and telling family and friends (55.77%); using the internet for marketing is 12.89%; the biggest challenge reported is 'too few customers' (60.10%). Informal businesses interact mainly with customers (61.89%) and almost never with NGOs or training institutions (86-88% report no contact). (https://hsrc.ac.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/IIS_StatisticalReport_Philippi_V5-WEB.pdf, 2025 (survey 2021-2022)) — Work and customers come through personal networks, with WhatsApp used by a third and Facebook by a quarter. An AI use case should amplify word of mouth (shareable voice/text 'cards', referrals) rather than replace it, and the top pain point is demand (finding customers/jobs), not credentials.
- South Africa job-seeking channels: The Citizen (Feb 2025) estimates ~850 WhatsApp job groups nationally, each with thousands of members, run free by volunteer admins who post listings for immediate reply, mainly entry-level retail/internship roles for people without laptops or data for job portals; admins vet posts and treat any 'admin fee' as a scam flag. Sowetan (May 2026) documents township youth earning as 'runners' (personal shoppers) by posting product videos on TikTok, Facebook and WhatsApp (one earns ~R3,500/month from a single client). Day labourers gather at ~1,000 open-air hiring sites (street corners, car parks) with ~45,000 workers (Blaauw estimate). SweepSouth's 2025 survey of 5,039 domestic workers (recruited via SMS/WhatsApp) found 85% work part-time for multiple employers, 39% earn below minimum wage, 72% cannot save, and 64% are Zimbabwean nationals. (https://citizen.co.za/news/south-africa/whatsapp-job-groups-offer-hope-to-south-african-job-seekers, 2025-2026) — Informal hiring in SA already runs through WhatsApp groups, social video and street corners, with multi-employer piece work the norm. Opportunities for AI: triage/verify postings in groups, help a worker answer a listing by voice within minutes, and give multi-employer workers one portable record.
- Nigeria (Jobberman/Mastercard Foundation 2025 report 'Technology and Employment Inclusion in Marginalised Contexts', via Techsoma and NAN coverage): 44% of women in traditional communities use WhatsApp for marketing and 31% for client acquisition; 45% of persons with disabilities rely on WhatsApp for job searches (reported 55% success); only 20-21% of job seekers find success via Facebook/Instagram; informal employers hire marginalised groups at 33.6% vs 27.6% of formal employers; 72% of formal employers make no inclusive-hiring effort; only 24% of rural women have internet access. The informal sector accounts for 76.7% of employed Nigerians (Jobberman/Mastercard Foundation). (https://techsoma.africa/nigerias-hidden-crisis-24-internet-access-exposes-digital-apartheid-keeping-millions-jobless/, 2025) — In Nigeria, informal employers, not formal ones, are the realistic hiring pool, and WhatsApp is already the job-search tool for excluded groups. Any job-matching AI should target small informal employers and work inside WhatsApp; note the figures come from press coverage of the report, not the primary document.
- Data affordability: Nigeria's NCC approved a 50% tariff rise in January 2025, lifting the 1GB floor price from N287.50 to N431.25 (advertised average ~N475-N587); data spend doubled to N585bn by March 2025 while usage dipped. ITU 2023 2GB mobile baskets: Nigeria $2.35, Kenya $2.92, South Africa $7.98. South Africa's data-only basket was 1.63% of monthly GNI per capita (~R152/month) in 2024, under the 2% Broadband Commission target, while Africa's median was 4.2% of GNI. In Kenya, low-income users buy time-boxed bundles (Safaricom B-Live KSh20-25/hour); Safaricom's CFO told investors that customers not using 1GB a month are 'a large opportunity' (commentators put this at roughly half of data subscribers), and 18.1 million informally employed Kenyans are paid per job rather than monthly, so they cannot pre-buy monthly bundles. (https://www.itedgenews.africa/kenyas-data-market-is-repricing-against-the-people-who-buy-small/, 2024-2026) — Users buy data in tiny, irregular increments tied to daily cash flow. Every AI interaction must be measured in kilobytes (text/short voice, no images or video), and the product should work when the user has no data at all (zero-rated, USSD, SMS, or a call-back).
- Mobile money / USSD are the universal 'apps'. Kenya FinAccess 2024 (CBK/KNBS/FSD; 20,087 interviews): mobile money uptake 82.3% of adults aged 16+, 52.6% use it daily (up from 23.6% in 2021); lack of a phone (50.3%) is the main barrier to mobile money; the government Hustler Fund was used by 28.9% (8.1m) with business people and casual workers dominating; 45.6% of the financially excluded are rural youth aged 18-25. Nigeria A2F 2023 (EFInA, 20,000+ respondents): formal inclusion 64%, exclusion 26%, agent use up from 4.4% (2018) to 54% (2023), mobile phone use 93%; USSD handled 252m transactions worth N2.19tn in H1 2024 but USSD volume is falling sharply (GTCO reported a 64% drop in H1 2025) as 2G declines. (https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FinAccess-Household-Survey-Launch-Presentation.pdf, 2024-2025) — Casual workers already transact daily on M-Pesa/USSD and, in Nigeria, through agents. Payment history is a usable proxy for work history (MTN SA already underwrites handset rent-to-own on mobile-money history), and USSD/agent networks are a proven fallback channel for feature-phone users.
- Literacy and language: World Bank adult literacy (15+) is 70.4% for Nigeria (2024) and 91.2% for South Africa (2024); Kenya's most-cited figure is 81.5% (2018; 85% men, 78% women). GSMA's Mobile Gender Gap Report 2025 shows only 35% of adult Nigerian women use mobile internet vs 52% of men. South Africa Census 2022: isiZulu is the home language of 24.4%, isiXhosa 16.3%, Afrikaans 10.6%, English only ~8-9%. Research on SMS practice in SA shows users text in abbreviated English but switch to voice or long-form isiXhosa for anything expressive, because isiXhosa is hard to type without predictive dictionaries. (https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/NG/indicator/SE.ADT.LITR.ZS?format=json&mrv=1, 2018-2025) — Roughly 3 in 10 Nigerian adults and 2 in 10 Kenyan adults cannot read a simple sentence, and over 90% of South Africans do not speak English at home. Text-first, English-first AI (including CV generators) will miss the target group; input and output need to be spoken, in isiZulu/isiXhosa/Swahili/Hausa/Yoruba/Pidgin, with English only as the employer-facing output.
- Zero-rating and the cost of looking for work: Airtel Nigeria zero-rates Yoma and the Nigeria Learning Passport under GenU 9JA; UNICEF/Airtel report Yoma visits grew from 5m to 20m+ with 95% of Yoma users paying no data charges (June 2025). In South Africa, ICASA's 2022 spectrum licence conditions oblige operators to zero-rate public-benefit-organisation websites by January 2027, but by August 2026 only 15-40 organisations had been onboarded (Rain ~two dozen) and implementation differs by network; Khayelitsha youth and the NYDA publicly demanded zero-rated data for job and NSFAS applications (Feb 2026). Vodacom has zero-rated a set of job portals (NXT LVL) since 2016. Median job-search cost for an unemployed young South African is R550/month (R350 transport, R200 data/print; CSDA Siyakha), with Youth Capital estimates of R1,000-R1,469/month. (https://dgmt.co.za/business-day-article-concern-over-slow-rollout-of-free-data-for-public-benefit-sites/, 2025-2026) — Yoma's existing zero-rating (strong in Nigeria, uneven in SA, unconfirmed in Kenya) is a real distribution advantage: a Yoma-backed WhatsApp/USSD assistant could be the data-free way to find and apply for work. Job search is a cash cost (transport + data) that an AI that pre-screens or confirms a job before travel would directly reduce.
